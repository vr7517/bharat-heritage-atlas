# Extracted Historical Claims: Sanchi Great Stupa & Archaeological Complex

Each claim below is atomized, classified by evidence strength, and linked directly to verified bibliographic sources. Historical consensus, material archaeology, epigraphy, and conflicting historiographical interpretations are recorded explicitly.

## Claim 1: Ashokan Mauryan Foundation of Brick Core Stupa (c. mid-3rd c. BCE)
* **Claim ID**: `CLAIM-SAN-01`
* **Claim Type**: `ARCHAEOLOGICAL_STRATIGRAPHY` / `CHRONOLOGY`
* **Statement**: The original core of the Great Stupa (Stupa 1) was founded by Mauryan Emperor Ashoka in the mid-3rd century BCE (c. 250 BCE) as a low hemispherical brick tumulus approximately half the diameter of the extant monument.
* **Consensus Status**: `SETTLED_CONSENSUS`
* **Evidence Classification**: `STRONG_EVIDENCE`
* **Evidence Typology**: `ARCHAEOLOGICAL` / `LITERARY`
* **Supporting Evidence**:
  * **EV-SAN-01 (Internal Brick Core Stratigraphy)**: 19th-century penetrations and Marshall's systematic conservation revealed the inner core composed of burnt mud and large Mauryan bricks ($40 \times 25 \times 7.5\text{ cm}$) measuring approx. $18\text{ m}$ in diameter, encased by the later stone structure.
  * **EV-SAN-02 (Ceylonese Chronicle Testimony)**: The *Mahavamsa* (XIII.6–11) records that Prince Ashoka while viceroy at Ujjain married Devi of Vidisha, who bore Mahendra and Sanghamitta, and that Devi built a monastery at *Chetiyagiri* (identified by Cunningham and Marshall with Sanchi).
* **Citing Sources**:
  * `[SRC-SAN-01]` Cunningham (1854), *The Bhilsa Topes*, pp. 180–190.
  * `[SRC-SAN-02]` Marshall & Foucher (1940), *The Monuments of Sāñchī*, Vol. I, pp. 21–25.
  * `[SRC-SAN-03]` Marshall (1918), *A Guide to Sanchi*, pp. 31–34.
* **Analysis**: Material stratigraphy inside the dome confirms that the monument began as an Ashokan imperial foundation.

## Claim 2: Ashokan Monolithic Pillar & Imperial Schism Edict
* **Claim ID**: `CLAIM-SAN-02`
* **Claim Type**: `EPIGRAPHIC` / `HISTORICAL_SYNCHRONISM`
* **Statement**: Emperor Ashoka erected a 42-foot polished monolithic sandstone column (Pillar 25 / Pillar 10) crowned by a four-lion capital near the southern entrance of Stupa 1, bearing the imperial *Sanghabheda* (Schism) Edict ordering the expulsion of sectarian dissenters from the Buddhist monastic community.
* **Consensus Status**: `SETTLED_CONSENSUS`
* **Evidence Classification**: `STRONG_EVIDENCE`
* **Evidence Typology**: `EPIGRAPHIC` / `ARCHAEOLOGICAL`
* **Supporting Evidence**:
  * **EV-SAN-03 (Brahmi Text of Schism Edict)**: A 20-line inscription in early monumental Brahmi script engraved on the polished shaft: *...saṃghe samage kate... Ye saṃghe bhokhati bhikhu vā bhikhuni vā se odātāni dusāni saṃnaṃdhāpayitu anāvāsasi nidhāpetaviye* ("The Sangha is made whole... Whosoever breaks the Sangha, monk or nun, shall be clothed in white and expelled").
  * **EV-SAN-04 (Chunar Stone & Mauryan Mirror Polish)**: Petrographic composition of fine-grained buff Chunar sandstone displaying standard Mauryan high polish identical to the Sarnath, Allahabad, and Rampurva pillars.
* **Citing Sources**:
  * `[SRC-SAN-07]` Hultzsch (1925), *Inscriptions of Asoka*, CII Vol. I, pp. 160–161, Plate VIII.
  * `[SRC-SAN-02]` Marshall & Foucher (1940), Vol. I, pp. 25–28, 373–375.
* **Analysis**: Serves as direct primary material proof of Ashokan imperial authority exercising juridical supervision over monastic cohesion at Sanchi.

## Claim 3: Shunga Period Doubling, Ashlar Masonry Casing & Stone Vedika (c. mid-2nd c. BCE)
* **Claim ID**: `CLAIM-SAN-03`
* **Claim Type**: `ARCHITECTURAL_STRATIGRAPHY` / `CHRONOLOGY`
* **Statement**: During the Shunga period in the mid-2nd century BCE (c. 150 BCE), the brick stupa was encased in local hammer-dressed sandstone masonry, doubling its diameter to $36.6\text{ m}$ ($120\text{ ft}$), and was provided with an elevated circumambulatory terrace (*medhi*), a *harmika*, a triple umbrella (*chhatravali*), and a monumental ground-level stone balustrade (*vedika*).
* **Consensus Status**: `SETTLED_CONSENSUS`
* **Evidence Classification**: `STRONG_EVIDENCE`
* **Evidence Typology**: `ARCHAEOLOGICAL` / `ARCHITECTURAL`
* **Supporting Evidence**:
  * **EV-SAN-05 (Carpentry-Style Stone Joinery)**: The ground *vedika* consists of monolithic octagonal pillars (*thaba*), lenticular crossbars (*suchi*), and rounded coping stones (*ushnisha*) fitted using carpenter-derived mortise-and-tenon and socket joinery without mortar.
  * **EV-SAN-06 (Brahmi Donative Paleography)**: Paleographic analysis of over 600 donative inscriptions on the railing pillars and crossbars exhibits Middle Brahmi characters characteristic of the 2nd century BCE (Shunga era).
* **Citing Sources**:
  * `[SRC-SAN-02]` Marshall & Foucher (1940), Vol. I, pp. 29–36.
  * `[SRC-SAN-05]` Bühler (1894), *Epigraphia Indica*, Vol. II, pp. 87–116.
  * `[SRC-SAN-06]` Lüders (1912), *List of Brāhmī Inscriptions*, Nos. 161–668.
* **Analysis**: The massive stone balustrade demonstrates how Indian timber construction traditions were faithfully translated into monumental stone architecture during the Shunga era.

## Claim 4: Satavahana Gateways (Toranas) & Vidisha Ivory Carvers Guild Patronage
* **Claim ID**: `CLAIM-SAN-04`
* **Claim Type**: `EPIGRAPHIC` / `SOCIO_ECONOMIC_HISTORY`
* **Statement**: The four monumental carved stone gateways (*toranas*) were added in the 1st century BCE under Satavahana hegemony, with the earliest Southern Gateway funded in part by the royal artisans of King Siri Satakarni and carved by the Guild of Ivory Carvers of neighboring Vidisha.
* **Consensus Status**: `SETTLED_CONSENSUS`
* **Evidence Classification**: `STRONG_EVIDENCE`
* **Evidence Typology**: `EPIGRAPHIC` / `ART_HISTORICAL`
* **Supporting Evidence**:
  * **EV-SAN-07 (Vidisha Ivory Carvers Inscription)**: Epigraph engraved on the middle architrave of the South Gateway: *Vedisakehi damtakārehi rūpakammaṃ kataṃ* ("Done by the ivory carvers of Vidisha").
  * **EV-SAN-08 (Satavahana Satakarni Inscription)**: Epigraph on the South Gateway recording a donation by *Ananda*, the son of *Vasithi*, foreman of the artisans (*avesanin*) of King Siri Satakarni (*Rāño Siri Sātakaṇisa*).
  * **EV-SAN-09 (Miniature Precision in Stone Relief)**: Intricate, densely packed, deep-undercut relief carving on the gateway architraves directly replicates fine ivory and woodcarving craft traditions.
* **Citing Sources**:
  * `[SRC-SAN-06]` Lüders (1912), Nos. 345 & 346, pp. 36–37.
  * `[SRC-SAN-02]` Marshall & Foucher (1940), Vol. I, pp. 342, 377; Vol. II, Plates 1–20.
  * `[SRC-SAN-08]` Dehejia (1997), *Discourse in Early Buddhist Art*, pp. 91–98.
* **Analysis**: Invaluable historical testimony demonstrating how specialized urban craft guilds (*shreni*) financed and executed monumental public religious monuments across medium boundaries.

## Claim 5: Strict Aniconic Representation of the Buddha in Torana Narrative Art
* **Claim ID**: `CLAIM-SAN-05`
* **Claim Type**: `ART_HISTORICAL` / `ICONOGRAPHY`
* **Statement**: Throughout the rich narrative reliefs of all four gateways of Stupa 1 (illustrating Jataka stories and the Life of the Buddha), the Buddha is represented exclusively through sacred aniconic symbols (footprints, umbrella, empty throne, wheel, stupa) and never in human anthropomorphic form.
* **Consensus Status**: `SETTLED_CONSENSUS`
* **Evidence Classification**: `STRONG_EVIDENCE`
* **Evidence Typology**: `ART_HISTORICAL`
* **Supporting Evidence**:
  * **EV-SAN-10 (Systematic Absence of Bodily Figure)**: In complex multi-figure scenes such as the Great Departure (*Mahabhinishkramana*), horse Kanthaka is depicted galloping with royal groom Channa holding an umbrella above an empty saddle, signifying the invisible presence of Prince Siddhartha.
  * **EV-SAN-11 (Symbolic Focal Points)**: The Enlightenment is indicated by an empty throne beneath the Bodhi tree; the First Sermon by the *Dharmachakra*; and the Parinirvana by a stupa surrounded by weeping deities.
* **Citing Sources**:
  * `[SRC-SAN-02]` Marshall & Foucher (1940), Vol. I, pp. 181–220; Vols. II & III.
  * `[SRC-SAN-08]` Dehejia (1997), pp. 101–138.
* **Analysis**: Sanchi represents the ultimate zenith of early Indian aniconic Buddhist art prior to the development of figurative Buddha statues in the 1st century CE.

## Claim 6: Primary Inscriptional Discovery of Disciples' Relics in Stupa 3
* **Claim ID**: `CLAIM-SAN-06`
* **Claim Type**: `EPIGRAPHIC` / `RELIC_ARCHAEOLOGY`
* **Statement**: Excavations by Alexander Cunningham inside Stupa 3 at Sanchi uncovered the authentic inscribed relic caskets of the Buddha's two chief disciples, Śāriputra and Mahā-Maudgalyāyana.
* **Consensus Status**: `SETTLED_CONSENSUS`
* **Evidence Classification**: `STRONG_EVIDENCE`
* **Evidence Typology**: `ARCHAEOLOGICAL` / `EPIGRAPHIC`
* **Supporting Evidence**:
  * **EV-SAN-12 (Inscribed Relic Caskets of Stupa 3)**: Cunningham's 1851 excavation exposed a central stone chest containing two steatite caskets inscribed in early Brahmi characters:
    * Box 1: *Sāriputasa* ("Of Sariputra") containing a human bone fragment and beads.
    * Box 2: *Mahā-Mogalānasa* ("Of Maha-Maudgalyayana") containing bone fragments.
* **Citing Sources**:
  * `[SRC-SAN-01]` Cunningham (1854), *The Bhilsa Topes*, pp. 295–299, Plates XXI–XXII.
  * `[SRC-SAN-09]` Schopen (1997), *Bones, Stones, and Buddhist Monks*, pp. 23–43.
* **Analysis**: Discovered in undisturbed primary context, these inscribed caskets provide definitive material confirmation of the historical veneration of the Buddha's foremost direct disciples in 2nd-century BCE Central India.

## Claim 7: Scholarly Debate — Shunga Religious Policy at Sanchi (Destruction vs. Continuity)
* **Claim ID**: `CLAIM-SAN-07`
* **Claim Type**: `HISTORICAL_ATTRIBUTION` / `RELIGIOUS_POLICY`
* **Statement**: A significant historiographical debate exists regarding whether early Shunga ruler Pushyamitra Shunga violently damaged the Ashokan stupa before it was rebuilt, or whether the enlargement represents continuous peaceful Buddhist patronage under Shunga rule.
* **Consensus Status**: `ONGOING_DEBATE`
* **Evidence Classification**: `SCHOLARLY_DEBATE`
* **Evidence Typology**: `SCHOLARLY_INTERPRETATION`
* **Conflicting Positions**:
  * **Position A (Damage and Rebuilding Hypothesis — Marshall, Divyavadana)**:
    * *Argument*: Later Buddhist texts (e.g., *Divyavadana*, *Ashokavadana*) portray Pushyamitra Shunga as an enemy of Buddhism who destroyed monasteries. John Marshall suggested that the Ashokan brick stupa was vandalized during Pushyamitra's reign and subsequently repaired and magnified under his successors (Agnimitra or later).
    * *Source*: `[SRC-SAN-02]` Marshall & Foucher (1940), Vol. I, pp. 23–24.
  * **Position B (Continuity and Absence of Archaeological Destruction Layer — Lamotte, Sircar, Singh)**:
    * *Argument*: Stratigraphic excavations reveal no evidence of a fire or deliberate iconoclastic destruction layer between the brick core and the sandstone casing. Furthermore, numerous contemporary Shunga-period railings and stupas (Bharhut, Sanchi Stupa 2) were lavishly sponsored by citizens and merchants living under Shunga rule without state interference.
    * *Source*: Étienne Lamotte, *History of Indian Buddhism* (1988); Upinder Singh, *A History of Ancient and Early Medieval India* (2008), pp. 382–384.
* **Resolution in System**: The database documents the Shunga era casing in `shunga` dynastic context with `is_dating_uncertain = false`, while registering the debate explicitly in claim and historiographical notes.
